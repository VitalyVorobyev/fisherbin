# A Higgs search with a floating energy scale

*Who this is for: a physicist who histograms a classifier output before fitting, and wants to know
what that histogram costs once a detector systematic is floating in the same fit.*

## The measurement

An H→ττ analysis is trying to measure one number: how much Higgs-to-tau-tau signal is present,
relative to what the Standard Model predicts. Nobody fits that number to individual collision
events. The events are reduced to a table of counts — how many candidate events landed in each of
a handful of categories — and the likelihood is built on that table. The categories are almost
always slices of a classifier output: train a network to separate signal from background, and
histogram what it says.

The complication is that the tau energy scale is not known exactly. Calibration leaves a residual
uncertainty, and a modern analysis handles that by letting the energy scale float in the fit as a
nuisance parameter, constrained but not fixed. That changes the question the histogram has to
answer. A category boundary that separates signal from background beautifully is not necessarily a
category boundary that survives having the energy scale floated alongside the signal strength: the
region where signal concentrates is also the region the energy scale moves events through, so some
of what those categories carry gets absorbed by the nuisance rather than by the parameter you care
about.

So the question is not "is the classifier good". It is: given that you may publish only a handful
of counts, and given that the energy scale will float, which handful?

## Where the events come from

The study runs on {f("nEvents")} events drawn from the {f("datasetName")} —

those same rows, so a shifted-up and a shifted-down copy of every event exist side by side and stay
row-aligned.

## Reading an event as three coordinates

ScoreQuant does not take events. It takes, for each event, one row saying how sensitive that event
is to each parameter being measured — one column per parameter. Three parameters are in play here:
the signal rate `mu_htautau`, the combined background rate `nu_background`, and the tau energy
scale `tes`.

Nobody has the likelihood of a detector in closed form, so those columns are not written down. They
are reconstructed from *density ratios*, which is the input route that only needs a classifier: the
sensitivity of an event depends on the ratio between the densities that generate it, and a
calibrated classifier estimates exactly that ratio. This example goes through that route twice, in
two different ways, because the three columns are not all the same kind of quantity.

```python
import numpy as np

import scorequant as sq
from examples.hep_classifier import SCHEMA, load_fixture

data = load_fixture()
nominal = data.features_at(1.0)

assert nominal.shape == (data.n_events, 28)
assert SCHEMA.parameters == ("mu_htautau", "nu_background", "tes")
assert SCHEMA.select("mu_htautau") == (0,)
```

The two **rate** columns come from an ordinary signal-versus-background classifier. Once the
signal-to-background density ratio is known, the sensitivity to a rate is closed-form algebra, so
nothing is differentiated numerically. The **energy-scale** column has no such closed form: `tes`
is a deterministic deformation of the features rather than a mixture weight, so the only way to
learn how the density responds to it is to compare a shifted-down sample against a shifted-up one.
A classifier trained to tell those two apart, at a half-offset of {f("delta")}, feeds the library's
central-difference door. That offset is a numerical differentiation step and not the physical
energy-scale uncertainty, which is smaller; the column it produces is the derivative at the nominal
scale whatever prior width the analysis later puts on it.

Both classifiers are cross-fitted, out of fold, with one fold identifier **per event** rather than
per row, so that an event's shifted-down and shifted-up copies never end up on opposite sides of a
fold boundary. That is not a detail. Most of the feature columns do not respond to `tes` at all, so
under a plain per-row split the classifier recognises the event from its inert columns and answers
with the label of the copy it was trained on — the wrong one — and the resulting column is
inverted while looking perfectly reasonable.

```python
from examples.hep_classifier.experiment import FOLD_SEED, HEADLINE_BINS, HEADLINE_DELTA, SOLVER_SEED
from examples.hep_classifier.scores import event_folds, fit_signal_background_oof, fit_tes_oof

fold_ids = event_folds(data.is_signal, n_folds=5, seed=FOLD_SEED)
signal = fit_signal_background_oof(data, fold_ids=fold_ids, max_iter=300, seed=FOLD_SEED + 100)
tes = fit_tes_oof(data, delta=HEADLINE_DELTA, fold_ids=fold_ids, max_iter=300, seed=FOLD_SEED + 500)
```

The weights are the other trap. Background events in this sample carry Monte Carlo weights orders
of magnitude above the signal's, so the weighted signal fraction is about
{f("signalFraction")}. Train on those raw weights and the signal class is statistically invisible.
Both classifiers are therefore trained on per-class normalized weights with declared priors of a
half each, and the physical rate ratio enters where it belongs — through the intensity
parameterization, never through the classifier's priors.

```python
rate_provider = sq.DensityRatioScore.from_classifier(
    lambda observations: signal.probabilities,
    [0.5, 0.5],
    sq.IntensityParameterization([1.0 - signal.signal_fraction, signal.signal_fraction]),
    calibration="temperature",
)
tes_provider = sq.CentralLogRatioScore(
    lambda observations: tes.probabilities,
    deltas=[tes.delta],
    class_priors=[0.5, 0.5],
)

rate_scores = np.asarray(rate_provider.score(nominal))  # columns [background, signal]
tes_scores = np.asarray(tes_provider.score(nominal))  # one column

sample = sq.ScoreSample(
    np.concatenate([rate_scores[:, [1, 0]], tes_scores], axis=1),
    data.weights,
    schema=SCHEMA,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(kind="estimated_ratio"),
)

assert sample.scores.shape == (data.n_events, 3)
assert sample.provenance.exact_fisher is False
```

That last assertion is the library refusing to flatter itself. These columns came from trained
classifiers, so they are estimates, and a sample that declares `estimated_ratio` provenance is not
permitted to report its output as exact Fisher information no matter how good the numbers look.

## Choosing the categories

With the table in hand the fit is one call. `optimize_partition` assigns each of the
{f("nEvents")} events to one of {f("bins")} categories, and the criterion says what "best" means:
`ProfiledDOptimality` asks for the categories that retain the most information about
`mu_htautau` *after* the background rate and the energy scale have been profiled out, which is the
question the analysis actually asks. `DExchangeConfig` is the exact finite solver — it moves one
event at a time, accepts only moves whose exact gain is positive, and therefore terminates rather
than converging approximately.

The one non-obvious argument is `initial_labels`. `efficient_score_bound` computes a certified
upper bound on what any {f("bins")}-cell rule of this space could retain, and it produces candidate
labels on the way. Starting the exchange from those labels is not a trick to make the number look
better; it is a starting point the bound already justifies, and the same bound then says how much
was left on the table.

```python
interest = SCHEMA.select("mu_htautau")

ceiling = sq.efficient_score_bound(
    sample.scores, interest=interest, weights=sample.weights, n_bins=HEADLINE_BINS
)
result = sq.optimize_partition(
    sample,
    n_bins=HEADLINE_BINS,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(("mu_htautau",)),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=SOLVER_SEED),
    initial_labels=ceiling.labels,
)

profiled = sq.profiled_information_report(
    sample.scores,
    np.asarray(result.labels),
    interest=interest,
    weights=sample.weights,
    n_bins=HEADLINE_BINS,
)

assert round(float(profiled.geometric_mean_retention), 4) == 0.9560
assert ceiling.gap_to(result) < 1e-3
```

## What that means for the signal strength

A table of {f("bins")} published counts carries {f("dsProfiledRetention")} of everything the full
event sample knows about the Higgs signal strength, with the tau energy scale floating. The
remainder is not sitting there waiting to be recovered by a cleverer rule: the certified ceiling at
that many cells is {f("ceilingRetention")}, and the fitted categories are {f("gapToCeiling")} below
it. The compression costs what it costs; the choice of compression, at this budget, no longer does.

The practical consequence is that the size of the published table becomes a design parameter you
can set on evidence rather than on convention. What one more category would buy is a measurement,
not a guess. You can run this shape of fit yourself, on your own table or on the study's, in
[the Lab](/lab?job=hep).

## What the histogram you would have drawn retains

The comparison has to be against something a physicist would actually do, and the honest version
of that is more than one thing. Slicing the classifier output into equal-frequency cells is the
first instinct. Slicing the *logit* of the same output into equal-width cells is the second, and on
this dataset it is considerably better. The two-bin significance cut — pick the threshold
maximising S/√B and count either side — is the most recognisable of all.

The significance cut retains nothing at all, and that is not a rounding artefact: two cells cannot
resolve three parameters, so once the background rate and the energy scale are profiled out there
is nothing left for the signal rate. The two multi-bin classifier binnings do much better, and the
strongest of them — this run records <code>{f("bestBaselineKey")}</code>, the {f("bestBaselineLabel")} — still
gives up about half. The headline gap is therefore {f("headlineGap")} of profiled information,
quoted against that strongest baseline.

The reason is structural rather than a matter of tuning. The classifier output is one number, built
to separate signal from background. No binning of it can encode a direction it never contained, and
the energy-scale direction is precisely the one it never contained.

## How much of that gap is the method

Here is the part worth taking away, and it cuts against the headline. Binning *the same classifier
posterior* two reasonable ways moves the retained profiled information by {f("baselineSpread")}.
Against the equal-frequency cells the gap reads {f("gapToEqualFrequency")}; against the logit
equal-width cells it reads {f("headlineGap")}. Both baselines are things people do. The difference
between them is larger than most method comparisons ever report, and it means a large part of any
such headline is a statement about how hard the baseline was tried, not about the method.

That is why this page quotes the smaller number. The claim that survives the stronger comparison is
still the interesting one — even a well-binned one-dimensional classifier output gives up around
half the profiled information — but a reader who takes {f("gapToEqualFrequency")} away from here
would be carrying a number that measures the weaker baseline's difficulty as much as anything else.

The second caveat is that the profiled criterion is a trade and not a free improvement. Against the
plain-D partition of the same table, the profiled partition gives up {f("fullRetentionGivenUp")} of
full three-parameter information to gain {f("profiledRetentionGained")} of profiled information. If
the energy scale were known exactly, or if the analysis wanted all three parameters equally, that
would be a bad trade. It is a good trade here because the analysis is measuring one parameter and
tolerating the other two, which is a statement about the analysis rather than about the criterion.
