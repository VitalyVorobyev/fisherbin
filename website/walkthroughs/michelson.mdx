# A Michelson interferometer read out through K counters

## The instrument

A Michelson interferometer does one thing with light: it splits a beam in two, sends the halves
down two arms, reflects them back and lets them recombine. If the two arms have exactly the same
length the halves arrive in step and add up. If one arm is longer by half a wavelength they arrive
out of step and cancel. Tilt one mirror slightly and the path difference varies across the beam,
so the recombined light shows alternating bright and dark bands, the *fringes*. Move a mirror by
a quarter of a wavelength and every fringe shifts by half a fringe width. The instrument turns a
length into a position on a screen, with the wavelength of light as its ruler.

/>

Albert Michelson built the first one in 1881 and, with Edward Morley in 1887, used it to look for
the motion of the Earth through the ether; the fringes refused to shift, and that null result is
part of the road to special relativity. The design has never gone out of use. A Fourier-transform
spectrometer is a Michelson interferometer with one mirror scanning; the gravitational-wave
observatories are Michelson interferometers with four-kilometre arms, and what they detect is a
change of fringe phase.

## What is measured

The quantity such an instrument exists to report is the *phase* of the fringe pattern: where the
bright bands sit, in units of the fringe period, which is the path difference in units of the
wavelength. Call it $\varphi$.

There is a complication, and it is not a decorative one. The *spacing* of the fringes depends on
the wavelength and the tilt geometry, and neither is known to the precision the phase is wanted
at. Write the fractional error of the fringe frequency as $\epsilon$. Over a short stretch of
pattern, a small phase offset and a small frequency error produce almost the same picture: both
slide the bands a little. So the fit cannot fix $\epsilon$ at its nominal value; it must let it
float and estimate both, and the phase estimate pays for that freedom. In the vocabulary of
estimation, $\varphi$ is the parameter of interest and $\epsilon$ is a nuisance parameter that
is *profiled out*.

Along the detector, write $u$ for the fringe phase coordinate: it advances by $2\pi$ per fringe
and runs over $m$ whole fringes. Conditioned on the number of photons, each photon's arrival law
is the fringe shape,

$$
p(u \mid \varphi, \epsilon) \;\propto\; 1 + V \cos\big((1+\epsilon)\,u + \varphi\big),
\qquad u \in [0, 2\pi m),
$$

with the visibility $V$, the contrast of the bands, fixed and known. On this page
$V$ is {f("visibility")} and the detector spans {f("fringes")} fringes. Every derivative below is
taken at the reference point $(\varphi_0, \epsilon_0) = (0, 0)$: the nominal fringe law, zero
offset and zero frequency error.

## The readout

A detector with K counters does not report where each photon landed. It reports K integers: how
many photons each counter collected. Everything downstream, the phase fit included, is built on
those K numbers. So the design question is: given K counters, and given that the fringe
frequency will float in the fit, which positions along the pattern should each counter collect
from?

The obvious answer is K equal segments, one counter per stretch of detector. It is also the
answer this page shows to be wrong, sometimes catastrophically. The alternative is not
restricted to contiguous stretches at all. A counter may be wired to collect from a *set* of
positions: a periodic mask in front of the detector, or a segmented sensor whose channels are
grouped electronically, does exactly that. The page therefore allows any assignment of positions
to K counters, disjoint unions included, and treats equal segments as the naive member of that
class and the baseline to beat. Hardware that can realise only contiguous segments faces a harder,
more constrained problem that this page does not solve; for such hardware the result here is a
ceiling on what any readout could do.

## What a photon tells you

A photon landing at fringe phase $u$ carries a certain amount of evidence about $\varphi$ and a
certain amount about $\epsilon$. The precise statement is its *score*, the derivative of the
log-likelihood at the reference point. Over whole fringes the normaliser of the law is the
interval length at $\epsilon_0 = 0$, so $\partial_\varphi \log Z = 0$ while
$\partial_\epsilon \log Z = V\cos\varphi_0$ does not vanish, and the score is closed-form:

$$
s_\varphi(u) = \frac{-V \sin u}{1 + V\cos u},
\qquad
s_\epsilon(u) = u\, s_\varphi(u) - V .
$$

The first component says where phase information lives: on the steep flanks of a fringe, where
the intensity is *changing*, and not at the bright crests or the dark troughs, where a small
shift changes nothing. The second is the same quantity multiplied by the position $u$, minus the
constant $V$. That constant is the derivative of the normaliser and it is what makes the
frequency score average to zero exactly; drop it and the information matrix is wrong. The
library never centres scores for this reason: the origin of score space is set by the model,
not by convenience, and this is the cleanest case of why.

Because the model is exact, the score enters the library as an analytic function.
`ScoreFunction` wraps the callback and declares what it is: an exact-model score, taken at the
stated reference point, so that nothing downstream mistakes it for an estimate.

```python
import numpy as np

import scorequant as sq

EXECUTION = sq.ExecutionConfig(backend="numpy", precision="float64", device="cpu")

VISIBILITY = 0.6
FRINGES = 4
U_MAX = 2.0 * np.pi * FRINGES

SCHEMA = sq.ScoreSchema(("phase", "fringe_frequency"))
INTEREST = SCHEMA.select("phase")


def fringe_density(observations):
    """The normalized one-photon arrival law along the fringe coordinate."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    return (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u)) / U_MAX


def michelson_score(observations):
    """Sensitivity to (phase, fringe_frequency) at the reference point, in closed form."""
    u = np.asarray(observations)[:, 0]
    s_phase = -VISIBILITY * np.sin(u) / (1.0 + VISIBILITY * np.cos(u))
    return np.column_stack([s_phase, u * s_phase - VISIBILITY])


provider = sq.ScoreFunction(
    michelson_score,
    provenance=sq.ScoreProvenance(kind="exact", reference_point=(0.0, 0.0)),
    schema=SCHEMA,
)
```

A sensitivity map alone says nothing about how often each fringe phase occurs; the fringe law
does. `IntegrationSource` carries that law as a bounded interval with an explicit density, and
a plain midpoint rule over the same interval gives the table the fixed-table task runs on:
{f("nNodes")} positions along the detector, each weighted by the fringe intensity there. There
is no sample, no seed and no Monte Carlo error anywhere on this page. Two entries of the
unbinned information matrix have closed forms, $I_{\varphi\varphi} = 1 - \sqrt{1 - V^2}$ and
$I_{\varphi\epsilon} = \pi m\, I_{\varphi\varphi}$, and the table reproduces both to machine
precision. On the other walkthroughs the library's numbers illustrate the mathematics; here they
check it.

```python
source = sq.IntegrationSource(
    [[0.0, U_MAX]],
    density=fringe_density,
    quadrature=sq.GaussLegendreConfig(order=256),
)

n_nodes = 8_000
step = U_MAX / n_nodes
u = (np.arange(n_nodes) + 0.5) * step
observations = u[:, None]
weights = fringe_density(observations) * step
scores = np.asarray(provider.score(observations, execution=EXECUTION))

information = np.asarray(sq.fisher_information(scores, weights, execution=EXECUTION))

# The two entries with closed forms, reproduced to machine precision.
assert abs(float(information[0, 0]) - (1.0 - np.sqrt(1.0 - VISIBILITY**2))) < 1e-12
assert abs(float(information[0, 1]) - float(information[0, 0]) * U_MAX / 2.0) < 1e-12
```

The numbers say how hard the problem is before any readout is designed. The phase information
per photon is {f("iPhiPhi")}. The correlation between the phase score and the frequency score is
{f("correlation")}: a phase offset and a frequency error are very nearly the same signal over
this baseline. Profiling the frequency out therefore costs {f("costOfProfiling")} of the phase
information *before a single counter is placed*. Nothing a readout does can win that back. What
the counters decide is how much of the remainder survives.

Every call on this page runs on the NumPy backend at double precision, declared once in
`EXECUTION` and passed explicitly; that is the portable runtime, and it runs the same
mathematics as the JAX default. The generating program is `examples/michelson_phase.py`, every
number here is read back from the evidence file it writes, and the full record with the
derivations is the <ReferenceLink to="examples/michelson-phase/">Michelson interferometer
phase</ReferenceLink> page of the reference.

## The objective

A readout is judged by one number: the phase information the K counts retain, *after* the
frequency has been profiled out, as a fraction of the unbinned profiled information. That is the
information a phase measurement actually feels. The criterion that optimises it is
`ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST)`. Plain `DOptimality` would optimise the information
about the pair $(\varphi, \epsilon)$ together, which is a different and easier question; the
difference turns out to be measurable.

The solver is exact finite relocation, `DExchangeConfig`: it moves one position at a time between
counters and accepts only strictly positive gains, so the result is stable against every single
move. Before it runs, `efficient_score_bound` computes a certified upper bound on what any rule
with K cells could retain from this score space, and its labels are the starting point. The bound
matters twice: as the ceiling the result is judged against, and as the reason the search starts
somewhere sensible.

```python
n_bins = 6

bound = sq.efficient_score_bound(
    scores, interest=INTEREST, weights=weights, n_bins=n_bins, execution=EXECUTION
)
result = sq.optimize_partition(
    scores,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=n_bins,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    initial_labels=bound.labels,
    execution=EXECUTION,
)


def profiled_retention(labels, n_bins):
    """Phase information retained, against the unbinned profiled ceiling."""
    return float(
        sq.profiled_information_report(
            scores,
            labels,
            interest=INTEREST,
            weights=weights,
            n_bins=n_bins,
            schema=SCHEMA,
            execution=EXECUTION,
        ).geometric_mean_retention
    )


def equal_width_labels(n_bins):
    """The naive readout: equal detector segments across the aperture."""
    return np.digitize(u, np.linspace(0.0, U_MAX, n_bins + 1)[1:-1])


assert round(profiled_retention(np.asarray(result.labels), n_bins), 4) == 0.9483
assert round(profiled_retention(equal_width_labels(n_bins), n_bins), 4) == 0.2054
```

The output, `result.labels`, is one counter index per position along the detector: the readout
map. `profiled_retention` is the yardstick, applied to every readout the same way. (The page
says *counters* where the library says bins or cells and the code says `n_bins`; they are the
same K labels.)

What the fitted readout looks like is the page's most transferable point. A photon's evidence
depends on its position only through its phase within a fringe, so a counter that is right for
one flank of one fringe is right for the same flank of every fringe. Each counter therefore
collects from one interval *per fringe*, and laid along the detector the readout is a comb: the
compiled plain-D rule occupies {f("combRuns")} separate intervals for {f("bins")} counters. When
the physics is periodic, the information-optimal readout is periodic, and a contiguous
segmentation is fighting the model rather than following it.

Two rows of that table are the lesson. At {f("sweepBinsLow")} counters, equal segments retain
{f("equalWidthRetentionAtFour")}: four equal segments over four whole fringes make each segment
exactly one period, so by periodicity every segment sees the same average photon and the counts
carry no phase information at all. At {f("sweepBinsTen")} counters, equal segments retain
{f("equalWidthRetentionAtTen")}, which is *worse* than the {f("equalWidthRetentionAtEight")} of
{f("sweepBinsEight")}: ten segments over four fringes is two and a half per fringe, which aliases
again, so buying more counters made the measurement worse. Both are the same effect, a
segmentation beating against the fringe period. Refining a readout helps only when the new cells
genuinely subdivide the old ones, and an equal segmentation at a new count does not subdivide
the old one; it is a different readout that happens to have more cells. The fitted readout, by
contrast, improves along the sweep and stays on or near the certified ceiling at every budget.

## What it means

**Where this applies.** Wherever the physics is periodic and the readout is free to be periodic
too: a periodic mask, a modulo-addressed segmented detector, an electronic grouping of channels.
For hardware that can only realise contiguous segments the result is a design ceiling; for
hardware that cannot tell the difference it is a design.

**Plain D is not the phase criterion.** At {f("sweepBinsLow")} counters a plain-D partition of
the same table retains {f("dOptimalRetentionAtFour")} of the phase information against the
profiled partition's {f("profiledRetentionAtFour")}; at {f("sweepBinsHeadline")} it is
{f("dOptimalRetentionAtSix")} against {f("profiledRetentionAtSix")}. Plain D is not misbehaving.
It is optimal for the pair of parameters, which is a different question from the one an
interferometer asks.

**The library refuses to turn the profiled readout into a reusable rule, and the refusal is a
result.** A D-optimal partition that is stable against every single move can be compiled into a
rule that labels new photons by a distance computation, without refitting. A profiled partition
cannot, and asking produces an error rather than an approximation:

```python
d_partition = sq.optimize_partition(
    scores,
    weights=weights,
    n_bins=n_bins,
    criterion=sq.DOptimality(),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
assert d_partition.exchange_stable is True
compiled = d_partition.compile_quantizer(execution=EXECUTION)

try:
    result.compile_quantizer(execution=EXECUTION)
    raise AssertionError("a profiled partition must refuse to compile")
except sq.RefusalError as error:
    refusal = str(error)

assert refusal.endswith("[CE-DS-GLOBAL-GEOMETRY-001]")
```

The message is <code>{f("refusalMessage")}</code>. The geometry that makes a stable D-optimal
partition equivalent to a distance rule does not hold for the profiled criterion, so there is no
rule to compile into; that is a theorem, not a missing feature. The compiled plain-D rule, scored
on a fresh set of positions it never saw, retains {f("compiledTestRetention")} of the
two-parameter information, which is the one held-out number on this page. A reusable
*profiled* rule has to be fitted as one, from the source directly:

```python
d_rule = sq.fit_quantizer(
    source,
    provider=provider,
    n_bins=n_bins,
    criterion=sq.DOptimality(),
    config=sq.DExchangeConfig(seed=4),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)
ds_rule = sq.fit_quantizer(
    source,
    provider=provider,
    n_bins=n_bins,
    criterion=sq.ProfiledDOptimality(interest=INTEREST),
    config=sq.SoftVoronoiConfig(
        seed=4, initializer_restarts=8, max_steps=300, record_every=37
    ),
    execution=EXECUTION,
)

# The comparable column: both rules' own labels, put through the same ceiling.
d_rule_phase = profiled_retention(np.asarray(d_rule.predict_scores(scores)), n_bins)
ds_rule_phase = profiled_retention(np.asarray(ds_rule.predict_scores(scores)), n_bins)
assert ds_rule_phase > d_rule_phase

# The column that does NOT compare: each rule scored on the criterion it optimized.
assert d_rule.train_report is not None
assert ds_rule.train_profiled_report is not None
```

**Two rules, two columns, and only one of them compares.** On phase information, the same
quantity against the same ceiling for both, the profiled rule retains
{f("dsRuleProfiledRetention")} against the plain rule's {f("dRuleProfiledRetention")}, and the
profiled rule wins, which is the entire reason it exists. Beside that sits each rule's score on
the criterion it actually optimised: {f("dRuleOwnCriterion")} for the plain rule and
{f("dsRuleOwnCriterion")} for the profiled one. Those two are *not* comparable: the first is the
overall two-parameter efficiency against the full unbinned information and the second is phase
retention against the profiled ceiling, and they happen to sit close enough to look like a
comparison. Reading that column alone was a real mistake made while this study was built, which
is why it is spelled out.
