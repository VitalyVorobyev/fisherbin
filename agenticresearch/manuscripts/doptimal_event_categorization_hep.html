HEP translation of the problem
Start from a high-dimensional reconstructed event \(x\). Around a reference theory point \(\theta_0\), compress it to a local score vector \(s(x)\). We then ask for a small number \(K\) of deterministic analysis categories such that the histogram of category counts retains as much joint local parameter information as possible. The D-criterion maximizes \(\det I_{\rm bin}\), equivalently minimizing the volume of the local Gaussian confidence ellipsoid when the full parameter vector is treated symmetrically.

Abstract

High-energy physics analyses routinely compress high-dimensional events into a small number of signal regions, categories, or histogram bins before likelihood fitting. When several theory parameters are constrained simultaneously, optimizing each category for a one-dimensional classifier score or a single significance proxy can discard information in parameter-space directions that are not aligned with the dominant separation axis. We study deterministic categorization after a local likelihood-score representation, \(s(x)=\nabla_\theta\log p(x\mid\theta)|_{\theta_0}\in\mathbb R^d\), and choose \(K\) hard categories to maximize the determinant of the Fisher information retained by the category label. The resulting binned information is the between-category covariance of the score, \(I_{\rm bin}=\sum_b W_b\mu_b\mu_b^\top\). For D-optimality, stationary categories form a self-consistent Mahalanobis-Voronoi tessellation in the metric \(I_{\rm bin}^{-1}\). On finite non-negatively weighted Monte Carlo samples, moving one event between categories produces an exact rank-two information update and a closed-form determinant gain. This yields a Hartigan-style exchange algorithm with exact monotone ascent and finite termination. A determinant-specific leverage bound further implies that every one-event exchange-stable solution is already a strict self-consistent D-Voronoi categorizer and is therefore directly deployable on unseen events. Global optima can be certified for moderate samples by branch-and-bound and found in polynomial time in sample size for fixed \((d,K)\) by affine-partition enumeration. We position the method relative to optimal observables, SALLY/SALLINO and MadMiner, INFERNO and inference-aware neural summaries, ThickBrick, and recent differentiable multidimensional binning. Particular emphasis is placed on HEP-specific scope: the strongest theorem applies to full D-optimality, while profiled \(D_s\) objectives for parameters of interest in the presence of nuisance parameters retain an exact move oracle but lose the exchange-stability-to-Voronoi implication; negative Monte Carlo weights and selection-dependent extended-rate information are outside the present finite-sample theorem.

1. From events to fit categories

A standard HEP analysis begins with a very high-dimensional event record and ends with a likelihood defined on a much smaller statistical summary. Even when a modern neural network enters the pipeline, the published or fitted object is often still a set of categories or binned templates. The question addressed here is not how to construct the best event representation from scratch, but how to turn a multi-parameter local inference representation into a small, hard, reusable category system without throwing away more Fisher information than necessary.

The mathematical contribution lies in the third arrow: optimal hard categorization once a score-space representation is available.

The score is especially natural in collider inference. In a regular model it is the gradient of the log likelihood at a reference point. Locally, it is the statistic that controls the likelihood-ratio expansion and Fisher information. This connects directly to the HEP language of optimal observables and to modern simulation-based inference methods that estimate or learn the score. Brehmer et al. explicitly exploit the score for local EFT inference, and MadMiner operationalizes this family of methods for particle-physics simulations. The present problem starts downstream of that representation: once every event can be mapped to a score vector, how should the score space be discretized into analysis categories?

Why hard categories at all?
Unbinned likelihood-ratio inference can be statistically stronger when a trustworthy likelihood-ratio surrogate is available. Hard categories remain useful because they integrate naturally with template fits, control-region machinery, public likelihoods, low-dimensional validation plots, finite-MC uncertainty treatments, and analysis preservation. The goal here is not to argue that binning is universally preferable, but to make the unavoidable or deliberately chosen binning step information-aware.

2. Positioning in HEP

2.1 Optimal observables and local scores

The HEP lineage closest in spirit begins with optimal-observable methods: for parameters entering a differential cross section smoothly, one constructs observables with locally maximal sensitivity and accounts for parameter correlations. In modern language, these objects are closely related to likelihood scores. Brehmer, Cranmer, Louppe and Pavez showed how simulator information can be used to learn likelihood ratios and scores for EFT analyses; SALLY/SALLINO-style local methods and MadMiner turn this into practical collider-inference workflows. Our method assumes this representation—or any valid approximation to it—is already available.

2.2 Inference-aware summaries and learned bins

HEP already has several methods that optimize summaries against inference objectives rather than classification loss. INFERNO constructs non-linear summaries by differentiating through an inference-motivated objective and explicitly considers nuisance parameters. Wunsch et al. optimize a differentiable binned Poisson likelihood with nuisance parameters. ThickBrick derives statistically motivated event selectors and categorizers with an iterative procedure connected to Lloyd-style clustering. Most recently, Learning to bin directly optimizes one- and multi-dimensional discriminant binning, including differentiable and Bayesian strategies. These works make broad novelty claims such as “inference-aware categorization” or “learned analysis bins” inappropriate here.

Conservative contribution statement
The narrower contribution is the optimization structure created by the conjunction of: (i) a multivariate local score representation, (ii) a small deterministic category alphabet, (iii) the full-matrix D-criterion \(\log\det I_{\rm bin}\), and (iv) exact finite-event reassignment. The strongest candidate novelty is the closed exact move algebra together with the determinant-specific theorem that one-event exchange stability forces a deployable self-consistent \(I_{\rm bin}^{-1}\)-Voronoi categorizer, plus the resulting exact/certifying algorithms. Historical priority still requires specialist literature review.

3. Statistical setup in analysis language

Let \(x\) denote a reconstructed event and \(\theta\in\mathbb R^d\) the parameter vector around a chosen reference point \(\theta_0\)—for example several Wilson coefficients, coupling modifiers, or continuous signal-model parameters. For the normalized event density \(p(x\mid\theta)\), define \[ s(x)=\nabla_\theta\log p(x\mid\theta)\big|_{\theta_0},\qquad E[s]=0,\qquad I_{\rm full}=E[ss^\top]. \] We restrict the final summary to a deterministic category label \[ Z=q(s)\in\{1,\ldots,K\}. \] For category \(b\), let \[ W_b=P(Z=b),\qquad m_b=E[s\,1_{Z=b}],\qquad \mu_b=E[s\mid Z=b]=m_b/W_b. \] The score of observing the category label is its conditional score mean \(\mu_Z\). The exact Fisher information retained by the category histogram is therefore \[ \boxed{I_{\rm bin}=\sum_{b=1}^K W_b\mu_b\mu_b^\top =\sum_{b=1}^K\frac{m_bm_b^\top}{W_b}.} \tag{1} \] Moreover, \[ I_{\rm full}-I_{\rm bin}=E[\operatorname{Cov}(s\mid Z)]\succeq0. \tag{2} \] So binning loses exactly the average within-category score covariance.

HEP interpretation of Eq. (1)
A good category is one whose events have a coherent score direction: events inside it pull the likelihood in similar directions in parameter space. If one category mixes events with strongly different score vectors, those differences are invisible after replacing all of them by one count and appear as within-bin information loss.

3.1 Rank: why a \(d\)-parameter fit needs at least \(d+1\) categories

Because \(\sum_bm_b=E[s]=0\), the \(K\) category moment vectors have one linear dependence: \[ \operatorname{rank}I_{\rm bin}\le\min(d,K-1). \tag{3} \] Thus an unregularized D-optimal objective requires \(K\ge d+1\). This is a useful analysis-design sanity check: a three-parameter local fit cannot retain full-rank shape information in only three mutually exclusive categories.

3.2 Rate information and extended likelihoods

Scope: category/shape information, not every term of an extended HEP likelihood
Equations (1)–(3) are written for a normalized per-event density and the categorical label of an observed event. In a standard extended likelihood, the total event yield may also carry parameter information. If all events are retained and the category mapping does not change the overall acceptance, the total-rate contribution can be treated separately and is not optimized by moving events among categories. If the analysis decision includes selection, acceptance, trigger efficiency, or otherwise changes the total yield, the corresponding extended-likelihood Fisher matrix must be included explicitly. That extension is not proved in this manuscript.

4. Why D-optimality for a multi-parameter fit?

For the full parameter vector, we optimize \[ F_D(q)=\log\det I_{\rm bin}(q). \tag{4} \] In the local asymptotic Gaussian approximation, parameter covariance scales as \(I^{-1}\), so \(\det I\) is inversely proportional to the squared volume of the confidence ellipsoid. D-optimality therefore rewards a categorization that retains information in all parameter-space directions rather than concentrating sensitivity along only the easiest direction.

Trace / whitened \(k\)-means

After Fisher whitening, maximizing normalized trace is exactly a weighted \(k\)-means problem. It rewards the sum of retained directional information.

Determinant / D-optimality

The determinant couples directions. Weakly constrained directions receive greater leverage because the current metric contains \(I^{-1}\).

This difference matters in HEP whenever the analysis is genuinely multi-parameter: e.g. two Wilson coefficients with partially degenerate kinematic effects, several Higgs coupling modifiers, or simultaneous measurements of signal components with correlated shapes. A one-dimensional discriminant or trace-optimal categorization can spend most of its resolution on the best-measured combination. The determinant penalizes that collapse.

5. What do optimal categories look like?

Consider transferring infinitesimal probability mass at score \(s\) from category \(a\) to \(b\). The first variation of the retained information is \[ dI_{\rm bin}=\big[(s-\mu_a)(s-\mu_a)^\top-(s-\mu_b)(s-\mu_b)^\top\big]d\varepsilon. \tag{5} \] For \(F_D(I)=\log\det I\), \(\nabla_I F_D=I^{-1}\), so population stationarity requires \[ \boxed{q(s)\in\arg\min_b (s-\mu_b)^\top I_{\rm bin}^{-1}(s-\mu_b)} \quad\text{a.e.} \tag{6} \] Thus the locally stationary analysis bins form a self-consistent Mahalanobis-Voronoi tessellation of score space. The boundaries are hyperplanes because the common quadratic term cancels when comparing two categories.

Euclidean / trace-like view

Whitened \(k\)-means uses a fixed Euclidean geometry.

D-optimal self-consistent metric

The metric adapts to the information matrix: weakly measured parameter combinations are stretched and receive more weight.

HEP-important consequence: terminal categories are deployable
For full D-optimality on a finite sample, the exact exchange-stability theorem below is stronger than population stationarity: once no positive one-event move exists, the observed category labels are reproduced exactly by the common rule in Eq. (6), with strict inequalities after duplicate score atoms are merged. Therefore the trained result is not merely a lookup table over one Monte Carlo sample. It can be serialized as category centroids plus the common metric \(I_{\rm bin}^{-1}\) and applied deterministically to new simulated or real events after their scores are computed.

6. Exact event relocation

For a finite sample of score vectors \((s_i,w_i)\) with non-negative weights, write each category mass and score moment as sums over its events. Suppose an event \((s,w)\) is moved from non-singleton source category \(a\) to destination category \(b\). Define \[ u_a=s-\mu_a,\qquad u_b=s-\mu_b,\qquad \alpha=\frac{wW_a}{W_a-w},\qquad \beta=\frac{wW_b}{W_b+w}. \tag{7} \] The entire information matrix changes by only \[ \boxed{\Delta I=\alpha u_au_a^\top-\beta u_bu_b^\top.} \tag{8} \] This is one positive and one negative rank-one update. Let \(H=I^{-1}\) and \[ q_{aa}=u_a^\top Hu_a,\quad q_{bb}=u_b^\top Hu_b,\quad q_{ab}=u_a^\top Hu_b. \] Then the exact objective change is \[ \boxed{\Delta F_D=\log\!\left[(1+\alpha q_{aa})(1-\beta q_{bb})+\alpha\beta q_{ab}^2\right].} \tag{9} \] No differentiable boundary parameterization and no approximate gradient through a histogram is required for this finite move.

HEP caveat: negative Monte Carlo weights are not covered
The finite theory treats \(w_i\) as probability/event-mass weights and uses positivity in the information and leverage arguments. Negative weights from NLO event generation break that interpretation and can invalidate positive-semidefiniteness and monotonicity statements. A practical HEP implementation must either operate on a non-negative representation of the target score law or develop a separate signed-measure treatment; simply feeding negative generator weights into Eq. (7) is not justified by the present theorem.

6.1 Monotone exchange solver

initialize K nonempty categories in score space
compute W_b, m_b, μ_b, I_bin and a Cholesky factor

repeat
    accepted = false
    for event i in a sweep:
        a = current category(i)
        skip if removing i would empty a

        evaluate exact ΔF_D(i: a → b) for every b ≠ a
        if best gain > tolerance:
            accept the best move
            update category moments and matrix factorization
            accepted = true
until a full sweep accepts no move

Every accepted move strictly increases the exact D-objective. Since a finite sample has finitely many hard labelings, the algorithm terminates in finitely many accepted moves at a one-event exchange-stable state. Multi-start initialization remains important because true local optima exist.

6.2 Why the obvious Lloyd iteration is unsafe

A tempting algorithm is: compute \(I^{-1}\), reassign all events to the nearest centroid in that metric, recompute the metric, and iterate. The assignment step improves the current tangent surrogate \(\operatorname{tr}(I_t^{-1}I)\), but because \(\log\det\) is concave its tangent is an upper bound rather than a minorizer. Therefore the batch step is not a monotone D-optimization algorithm. The numerical test suite contains explicit counterexamples; one rounded \(N=8,d=2,K=3\) case loses \(0.1365\) nat in one batch step.

7. The determinant-specific stability theorem

The key finite-sample structural result is stronger than the first-order statement. A leverage inequality gives \[ (\mu_a-\mu_b)^\top I^{-1}(\mu_a-\mu_b)\le \frac1{W_a}+\frac1{W_b}. \tag{10} \] Combining this bound with the exact determinant ratio shows that if an event is at least as close to destination \(b\) as to its current category \(a\) in the self-consistent D-metric, then moving it produces a strictly positive finite gain (except for the handled degeneracies).

Theorem — one-event stability implies D-Voronoi geometry
If an admissible move \(a\to b\) satisfies \[ (s-\mu_a)^\top I^{-1}(s-\mu_a)\ge (s-\mu_b)^\top I^{-1}(s-\mu_b), \] and \(\mu_a\ne\mu_b\), then \[ \Delta F_D\ge \log\!\left(1+\frac{\alpha\beta}{4}q_\delta^2\right)>0, \qquad q_\delta=(\mu_a-\mu_b)^\top I^{-1}(\mu_a-\mu_b). \tag{11} \] Hence every one-event exchange-stable finite D solution is a strict self-consistent \(I^{-1}\)-Voronoi categorizer after duplicate score atoms are merged. The converse is false: a Lloyd fixed point can still have a profitable exact move.

For an analysis implementation this theorem closes an important gap between optimization on a training Monte Carlo sample and definition of categories for future events. The exchange solver is free to pass through arbitrary assignments, but its terminal full-D state necessarily admits a common geometric decision rule.

8. Parameters of interest and nuisance parameters

HEP rarely treats every parameter symmetrically. Let \(\theta=(\psi,\lambda)\), where \(\psi\) are parameters of interest and \(\lambda\) nuisance parameters. The profiled Fisher information for \(\psi\) is the Schur complement \[ I_{\rm eff}=I_{\psi\psi}-I_{\psi\lambda}I_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}I_{\lambda\psi}, \qquad F_{D_s}=\log\det I_{\rm eff} =\log\det I-\log\det I_{\lambda\lambda}. \tag{12} \] Its first-variation metric is \[ G_s=I^{-1}-E_\lambda I_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}E_\lambda^\top\succeq0, \tag{13} \] which corresponds to the efficient score after projecting out nuisance directions.

The strongest D theorem does not survive profiling
The same rank-two \(\Delta I\) still gives a cheap exact finite move oracle for \(D_s\): the gain is a difference of two small determinant-lemma evaluations, one for the full matrix and one for the nuisance block. Exact positive-gain exchange therefore remains a sensible monotone optimizer. However, the implication “exchange-stable \(\Rightarrow\) self-consistent criterion-Voronoi” is false for \(D_s\) (and for A-optimality). The research suite contains explicit counterexamples where the first-order profiled metric favors a move but the exact profiled log-determinant decreases. For nuisance-dominated HEP fits, deployable geometric closure therefore requires additional algorithmic machinery or a separately parameterized categorizer.

This distinction is central for HEP interpretation. The full-D results are strongest for a genuinely joint local measurement problem. When the publication target is a small set of POIs profiled against many systematics, the profiled objective is arguably more relevant physically, but some of the elegant finite geometry is lost. INFERNO and related differentiable likelihood approaches remain particularly relevant comparators in that regime.

9. HEP use cases

Use case A

Local multi-coefficient SMEFT fits

Near the Standard Model point, the event score with respect to Wilson coefficients is a natural local optimal-observable representation. A high-dimensional event can be mapped to a \(d\)-component score, then compressed into a handful of categories whose determinant-optimal geometry tries to preserve the full joint EFT constraint rather than the best-measured coefficient combination.

Use case B

Simultaneous coupling or signal-component measurements

Analyses separating multiple Higgs production modes, coupling modifiers, or overlapping signal components often use several classifier outputs and hand-designed categories. A score vector for the simultaneous parameterization provides a principled coordinate system in which to optimize those hard categories.

Use case C

Compact, publishable likelihood summaries

Even when an unbinned ML estimator is available internally, collaborations may want a small set of stable categories or public templates for validation, reinterpretation, or long-term preservation. D-optimal score categories offer a way to choose that compression with an explicit local information-loss objective.

Use case D

Monte-Carlo-limited multidimensional template design

Fine multidimensional histograms quickly become sparse. The present method targets the opposite regime: very few hard cells in a physics-motivated local coordinate system. It can therefore be used to ask how much information a coarse template can retain before finite-MC uncertainties and systematic modeling dominate.

9.1 Where this is not the obvious objective

For pure discovery optimization of a single signal against background, significance-oriented categorizers such as ThickBrick may align more directly with the test statistic of interest. For a single POI with many nuisance parameters, a profiled variance objective is more natural than full D. For strongly non-local inference far from \(\theta_0\), one local score tessellation may not be sufficient; parameterized likelihood-ratio methods or multiple reference points may be preferable. The method is best viewed as a local multi-parameter categorization tool, not a universal replacement for HEP analysis optimization.

10. How does a HEP analysis obtain the score?

The categorizer only needs a mapping from an event to a \(d\)-component local score. The mathematical optimization is agnostic to how that oracle is implemented. Several HEP-relevant routes are possible:

Analytic / differentiable model

Evaluate the score from a tractable likelihood or matrix-element-inspired model at \(\theta_0\).

Simulator-augmented learning

Use joint score / likelihood-ratio information exposed by the simulator, as in the modern SBI / MadMiner lineage, to train a score estimator.

Classifier-backed proxy

Use a calibrated local density-ratio construction or parameterized classifier as a proxy score oracle. Raw class probabilities are not generically equal to the score.

A classifier-backed score oracle is therefore an implementation pathway, not part of the D-optimality theorem itself. Its accuracy controls how closely the optimized categories correspond to the true likelihood score. This separation is useful architecturally: the same category optimizer can consume exact score functions, precomputed score arrays, simulator-based estimators, or learned proxies.

11. Global optimization and certificates

Exact exchange is a local optimizer. The D-specific geometry nevertheless constrains the global problem. Every global optimum is exchange-stable and therefore strictly realized by \(K\) affine score functions, \[ B_b=\{s:v_b^\top s+t_b>v_c^\top s+t_c\ \forall c\}, \quad v_b=2I^{-1}\mu_b,\quad t_b=-\mu_b^\top I^{-1}\mu_b. \tag{14} \] For fixed score dimension \(d\) and number of categories \(K\), only \(N^{O(Kd)}\) strict labelings are realizable by such a multiclass affine partition, yielding an exact XP algorithm polynomial in \(N\) for fixed \((d,K)\).

For moderate finite samples, a more practical certificate comes from refinement monotonicity. For a partial assignment with unassigned event set \(U\), treating every unassigned event as its own singleton yields the upper bound \[ I_{\rm completion}\preceq I_{\rm partial}+\sum_{i\in U}w_i s_is_i^\top, \tag{15} \] so the log determinant of the right-hand side is a valid branch-and-bound upper bound. With a strong incumbent from multi-start exchange, the supplied numerical suite certified \(K=3,d=2\) instances through \(N=40\). These are instance-dependent results, not a claim of general scalability.

12. Population categories and Monte Carlo learning

At population level the hard categorizer acts on the score distribution itself. Relaxing to randomized assignments \(p_b(s)\) changes only the finite set of category masses and first score moments. For atomless score distributions, the Dvoretzky-Wald-Wolfowitz purification principle implies that every randomized solution can be replaced by a deterministic measurable partition with exactly the same \((W_b,m_b)\), and hence exactly the same Fisher objective. Under regularity assumptions, the first-order boundaries are hyperplanes of measure zero, so an optimal representative can be taken to have the self-consistent D-Mahalanobis geometry of Eq. (6).

For HEP, the population problem is the conceptual target while Monte Carlo provides an empirical approximation. The atomic nature of a finite MC sample creates technical questions that disappear in a continuous score law. In particular, splitting identical score atoms corresponds to randomized labeling and is not covered by classical atomless purification. The current experiments found no advantage from the relaxed atomic optimization, but this is not a proof.

14. A practical HEP workflow

1. Choose a local reference point θ₀ and the parameter vector to preserve.
2. Obtain a score oracle s(x) for simulated events.
3. Use non-negative event weights representing the target score law.
4. Center / Fisher-whiten scores for numerical conditioning.
5. Choose K ≥ d+1 for unregularized full D.
6. Initialize with several whitened k-means++ and random starts.
7. Refine every start with exact D exchange.
8. Keep the best exact objective; optionally test 2-swaps / merge-split moves.
9. Serialize centroids and I_bin^{-1} as the category rule.
10. Validate category occupancies, score moments, MC stability, and physics systematics.
11. For small benchmark subsamples, run branch-and-bound to quantify heuristic gaps.
12. If nuisance profiling is the actual objective, switch to D_s move gains and do not
    assume the full-D Voronoi closure theorem.

15. Discussion

15.1 What this changes relative to standard category optimization

The usual HEP instinct is to produce one or several discriminants and then tune thresholds or bins. The score-space formulation moves the physics objective earlier: the coordinates themselves represent local parameter derivatives, and the binning criterion is the information matrix of the final categorical observation. D-optimality then supplies a clear answer to the multi-parameter trade-off: preserve the volume of information, not merely the best one-dimensional separation.

15.2 What remains open for a publication-quality HEP method

  • Extended likelihood: integrate total-rate information, selections, and category-dependent efficiencies into the exact theory.
  • Nuisances: develop a deployable finite categorizer with stronger guarantees for profiled \(D_s\) or other HEP-relevant objectives.
  • Negative weights: determine a principled treatment of signed NLO event weights.
  • Population consistency: prove when empirical exchange or global solutions converge to population-optimal categories.
  • Score estimation: quantify category degradation under learned or classifier-backed score errors.
  • Real HEP benchmark: compare against standard classifier binning, INFERNO-like nuisance-aware optimization, ThickBrick-style categorization, and modern multidimensional learned binning on a public EFT or multi-signal task.
  • Historical novelty: continue adversarial prior-art search, especially determinant-based quantizer design and event-category optimization under different terminology.

16. Conclusion

For a local multi-parameter HEP measurement, a hard analysis category can be viewed as a compressed observation of the event score. This makes the Fisher information retained by a histogram explicit and turns category design into a well-defined matrix optimization problem. Under the full D-criterion, the finite problem has unusually strong structure: a one-event relocation changes the information by rank two, its exact determinant gain is available in closed form, positive-gain exchange is monotone, and every exchange-stable solution is already a self-consistent Mahalanobis-Voronoi categorizer that can be applied to new events. The result sits naturally between continuous score-based inference and conventional binned template fits: it does not replace either, but provides a principled way to build the hard interface between them.

For HEP the most important qualification is equally clear. The elegant closure theorem belongs to full D-optimality. Real analyses often profile nuisance parameters, use extended Poisson likelihoods, and contain signed Monte Carlo weights. The exact exchange machinery extends further than the geometry theorem, but these ingredients define the next technical layer required before the method can be advertised as a general-purpose collider-analysis optimizer.
